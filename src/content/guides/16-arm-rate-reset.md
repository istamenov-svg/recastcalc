## The short version

An **adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM)** has a fixed rate for its first few years (five years on a 5/1 or 5/6 ARM), then the rate adjusts. Many borrowers who took ARMs in 2020 and 2021 locked in very low starting rates, and those loans are now reaching their first adjustment at a time when rates are much higher.

Your new rate isn't today's advertised mortgage rate. It's calculated from three things in your loan documents:

1. **The index:** a published market rate your loan follows. Most newer ARMs use the Secured Overnight Financing Rate (SOFR).
2. **The margin:** a fixed amount added to the index, set when you got the loan.
3. **The caps:** limits on how much your rate can rise at the first adjustment, at each later adjustment, and over the life of the loan.

The [ARM reset calculator](/arm-reset/) runs these numbers for you.

## How your new rate is calculated

The starting point is the **fully indexed rate**: index plus margin. If the index is 3.9% and your margin is 2.75%, your fully indexed rate is 6.65%.

Then the caps apply. ARM caps are usually written as three numbers, such as **2/1/5**:

- **2: the initial adjustment cap.** At the first reset, your rate can rise no more than 2 percentage points above your starting rate.
- **1: the periodic cap.** At each later adjustment, it can rise no more than 1 point.
- **5: the lifetime cap.** Your rate can never be more than 5 points above your starting rate.

Your new rate is the **lowest** of: the fully indexed rate, your starting rate plus the initial cap, and your starting rate plus the lifetime cap. Your loan documents (the note, and the ARM disclosure you received) list your index, margin, caps, and adjustment schedule.

## A worked example: a 2021 ARM at its first reset

These numbers are illustrations, not your loan. Check your own documents for your actual margin, caps, and index.

Say you took a $400,000, 30-year ARM in mid-2021 with a 5-year fixed period at **2.75%**. Your payment has been about **$1,633** a month, and after five years you owe about **$354,000**.

At the reset, assume the index is about **3.9%** (the New York Fed's 30-day average SOFR was [VALUE] on [DATE]) and your margin is **2.75%**, for a fully indexed rate of about **6.65%**.

| Cap structure | New rate at first reset | New payment (25 years left) | Increase |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2/1/5 caps | 4.75% (capped at 2.75% + 2) | about $2,018 | about $385 a month |
| 5/2/5 caps | 6.65% (fully indexed, below the 5-point cap) | about $2,424 | about $791 a month |

**The first cap delays the increase; it doesn't prevent it.** With 2/1/5 caps on a loan that adjusts every six months (common for SOFR ARMs), the rate in this example would rise to 4.75%, then 5.75% six months later, then about 6.65% after that, if the index stays where it is. Plan for the fully indexed payment, not just the first step.

## If your ARM used LIBOR

Many ARMs from before 2022 were tied to LIBOR, an index that stopped being published in mid-2023. Under a 2022 federal law, the Adjustable Interest Rate (LIBOR) Act, contracts without a workable fallback were switched to a SOFR-based replacement rate selected by the Federal Reserve, including a fixed spread adjustment. Your servicer should have notified you of the change; if you're unsure what your loan's index is now, ask.

## Your options before and after the reset

**1. Pay down principal before the reset.** When an ARM adjusts, the new payment is generally recalculated on your remaining balance. Paying down principal before the reset date means the higher rate applies to a smaller balance. Ask your servicer how it applies extra payments around an adjustment. See [how a mortgage recast works](/guides/how-mortgage-recast-works/) for the mechanics of lowering a payment with a lump sum.

**2. Refinance into a fixed rate.** If you want certainty, a fixed-rate refinance ends the adjustments. Compare the fixed-rate payment with your fully indexed ARM payment, not just the first capped step, and include closing costs. Refinancing tends to make sense when the monthly savings repay the closing costs before you'd sell or refinance again.

**3. Keep the ARM.** If the index falls, your rate can fall too, and some borrowers expect to sell before the rate rises much. That's a bet on future rates; make sure you could afford the payment at your lifetime cap.

**4. Cut other housing costs.** Shopping your insurance and checking your property tax assessment can offset part of the increase. See [how to lower your mortgage payment without refinancing](/guides/lower-mortgage-payment-without-refinancing/).

**5. Ask for help early if you can't afford it.** If the new payment is unaffordable, contact your servicer before you miss a payment, and consider a free housing counselor approved by the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD).

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I know when my ARM resets?

Your loan documents list the first adjustment date. Federal rules also require your servicer to send you a notice before the first rate adjustment, giving you time to plan.

### Will my rate go up to today's mortgage rates?

Not exactly. Your new rate is your index plus your margin, limited by your caps. It can be higher or lower than advertised rates for new loans.

### Can my rate go down at the reset?

Yes, if the fully indexed rate is below your current rate. Many ARMs also have a floor, often equal to the margin, below which the rate can't fall. Check your note.

### What's the most my payment can go up?

Use your lifetime cap: calculate the payment at your starting rate plus the lifetime cap. That's the worst case under your loan's terms.
